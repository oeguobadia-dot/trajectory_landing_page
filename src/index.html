Why you can trust the numbers

Every number comes with the source. If we can't verify it, we leave it blank.

Every part of a map carries a tag saying what kind of thing it is. Data is a figure from a public federal table, with its vintage and a link. AI is text the model wrote — including how long it thinks each stage takes, which is the one estimate it's allowed. Computed is arithmetic over the two. You never have to guess which one you're looking at.

What you get in every career map

Know what to do next, what it will take, and which other paths stay open.

  1. Know your next step — not just your end goal

    See the courses, credentials, exams, and stepping-stone jobs in order, with the prerequisites for each one so you know what to act on next.

  2. See backup routes before you need them

    Career plans change. The map shows adjacent roles and off-ramps your training can lead to, so one change does not make the work you already did feel wasted.

  3. Compare pay and opportunity where you live

    Switch from national figures to state-level data where available, so the decision reflects the market you may actually enter rather than a generic national average.

  4. Compare careers without opening ten tabs

    Put two or three careers on the same scorecard and compare pay, growth, education, time to entry, typical costs, and day-to-day work in one view.

Why TrajectoryAI exists

Career advice needs judgment. Career decisions also need numbers you can verify.

Asking a chatbot

It answers in three seconds and sounds certain doing it.

A language model produces numbers the same way it produces sentences: by predicting what ought to come next. Sometimes that lands on the real figure. Nothing in the answer tells you which times it did.

Good for talking the idea through, drafting, and naming options you didn't know existed.

A counselor or a coach

A person who reads you, not your inputs.

They hold you accountable, know your school, and catch what no dataset records. They're also rationed. ASCA recommends 250 students per counselor; the national average is 372 to 1, and 571 to 694 in elementary and middle schools — which is where an 8th grader asking about astronauts actually sits.

Questions

The things people ask
before they sign up.

What does it cost?
Free while in beta, and no card. Each person gets a set number of maps to build during beta so we can watch how they're used; comparing careers side by side doesn't count against it. We'll say what the paid plan looks like well before anything changes.
Where do the numbers actually come from?
Wages and how many people hold a role: BLS Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics. Projected openings: BLS Employment Projections. Completion rates: NCES and IPEDS. What a job involves: O*NET, from the Department of Labor. Employer-filed wages: DOL H-1B disclosures. Education debt by stage: AAMC, ABA, NSF and NCES. All public, so any figure can be looked up. The one thing the model does estimate is how long each stage takes — and that's labelled AI on the map, not Data.
What if there's no data for my goal?
The field renders as unavailable and says so. Astronauts, military officers and many specialised government roles have no occupation code of their own — which is exactly why the astronaut map above carries no wage for its goal instead of a number we made up. Where a close published occupation exists, the map can show it, clearly labelled as a substitute. Where a path contains a step the data can't price, the earnings view says "no realistic salary path" and names the step, rather than showing a zero.
How current is the data?
Federal data lags. Wage tables are published annually and reflect their survey period, not this morning, so fast-moving fields may have moved. Figures are national and cross-industry unless the map says otherwise — the same job pays very differently by region and employer. And very small occupations are sometimes withheld by BLS because the sample is too thin to publish safely, so a gap can be the data protecting itself rather than an error. A path is a route people have taken, not a prediction.
What do I need to give it?
A goal, and where you are now. That can be a sentence — "I'm in 8th grade" — or a pasted transcript or an uploaded résumé, from which the map works out its own starting point. Uploads are read to find that point and aren't kept.
Is this only for students?
No. It works from middle school through a mid-career change. The map starts from wherever you tell it you are.
How old do you have to be?
If you're under 13 we'll ask for a parent or guardian's email instead of yours, and we write to them rather than to you.
What happens to my email?
One email, when your cohort opens. It isn't sold, shared, or used for ads.
